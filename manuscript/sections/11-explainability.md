# 10. Explainability and Decision Intelligence Layer

A policy the operator cannot inspect is a liability, so AdaSpark treats explainability as a layer rather than a paragraph. Fig. 15 shows its explainable-RL path (policy store, agreement check, replicate reporting, logged refusal) and Fig. 16 the decision-intelligence workflow that scores confidence, then decides or discloses, traces and audits. Every decision is traceable: the acting policy is a named checkpoint in the policy store (e.g., `b801f4a7df200b04`, 84 episodes, ε = 0.05), its Q-table is a plain JSON mapping states to action values, and the manifest behind it records code version, seeds, and cell coverage. No decision is ever a black-box output — the greedy action for any state can be read off the table and checked against the evidence density behind it (episodes per state per seed).

Confidence is quantified, not asserted. Equation (4) defines the decision-confidence score as cross-seed greedy-policy agreement — the fraction of evidence-bearing states on which independently trained replicates choose the same action:

conf(s) = 1[a*_1(s) = ⋯ = a*_k(s)],   C = (1/|S_e|)·Σ_{s∈S_e} conf(s)   (4)

Here a*_i(s) = argmax_a Q_i(s,a) is replicate i's greedy action in state s, k the number of replicates (three), and S_e the set of states visited in every replicate's training run. Measured at C = 0.20 against a 0.70 threshold (M8, FAIL), this score did exactly what a confidence measure should do: it blocked the promotion of a single "winner" and forced the honest reporting of three replicate arms (DEC-015). Explainability here is therefore not visualization alone but decision intelligence — the system refusing to claim more than its evidence supports, with the refusal itself recorded.

The explainable-RL literature separates explanations of a whole policy from explanations of single decisions, and feature-, policy- and outcome-oriented techniques from one another [101][102]. It argues for explanations that integrate several sources into a coherent account of an agent's behaviour, matched to the needs of whoever receives them [103]. Post-hoc attribution over a deep Q-network is one route [104]; inherently interpretable policies are another, and tabular RL has been chosen for high-stakes planning precisely because its policies can be read directly [98]. In power-distribution reconfiguration, transparency is reported as the precondition for operators to trust RL at all [105], and Industry 4.0 surveys make the same point for AI in production [106].

AdaSpark sits at the interpretable end of that spectrum. Each decision can be explained at three levels without any post-hoc model: the policy level (the Q-table row for the current state), the evidence level (how many episodes back each Q-value) and the outcome level (the manifest of the executed configuration). Decision intelligence adds the confidence score of (4) and the decision log, which records when the system declined to promote a policy. Policy visualization needs no separate model either: the greedy-action table is the policy, and Figs. 23 and 24 visualize its training dynamics and its per-family performance against the baselines. The same transparency exposed this study's most important structural limitation — on TEST the policy reduces to five per-family rules (Section VII) — a fact a reader of the Q-tables can verify in minutes and a black-box policy would have hidden. Trustworthy-ML guidance asks for exactly this inspectability and documented limitation [92].
